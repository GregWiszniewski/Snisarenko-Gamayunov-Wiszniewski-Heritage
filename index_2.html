Introduction

This site preserves the story of one family's passage through the upheavals of the twentieth century — from Soviet Ukraine through forced labour in wartime Germany, partisan resistance in Belgium, the quiet construction of entirely new identities, and emigration to Australia. It is told primarily through a rare collection of original documents spanning the late 1930s to the early 1980s, held by the family and transcribed here for the first time.

The grandparents at the centre of this story were born as Vladimir Snisarenko (Владимир Снисаренко) and Yevdokia Matveevna Gamayunova (Евдокия Матвеевна Гамаюнова) in the Kirovograd region of Ukraine. By the end of the war, they had become Victor and Stefa Wychenewsky, Polish-born Greek Catholics from Krakow. They would eventually settle in the Sydney suburb of Georges Hall as the Wiszniewski family.

No single document bridges these two identities — that was the point. The link rests on family testimony and on private photographs inscribed in Russian with their original names, kept hidden throughout their lives. Together, the public documents and private inscriptions form an unusually complete record of wartime identity reinvention.

Kirovograd: The World Before

Yevdokia Matveevna Gamayunova was born on or around 14 February 1921 in the Kirovograd region of Soviet Ukraine. Her mother was Darya Andreevna Gamayunova (Гамаюнова Дарья Андреевна), born 18 March 1898, who would live until 19 December 1983. A gravestone in Kirovograd, bearing the dedication "от дочери и сестры" — "from daughter and sister" — confirms the maternal connection and hints at a still-unidentified sister on Darya's side of the family.

A 1960 studio portrait of Darya bears the caption "Дом отдыха 'Красная звезда' г. Кировоград 1960 г." — the "Krasnaya Zvezda" rest home in Kirovograd — placing her there late into life and confirming the family's deep roots in the city.

Of Vladimir Snisarenko's origins, less is known from the surviving documents. The partisan demobilisation record and commendation letter describe him as Russian. He appears to have been older than Yevdokia, possibly born around 1916 based on the dates used in his later cover identity.

The earliest document in the collection is Yevdokia's Soviet passport, issued in February 1939 — the last snapshot of a life that was about to be swept away.

Soviet Passport — ОП № 595356

Issued 18 February 1939 by the 3rd Department, RK Militia, Kirovograd. This internal passport identifies the holder by patronymic and family name consistent with Gamayunova. The birth certificate number is tentatively read as 197486. It is the only surviving Soviet-era identity document in the collection.

Wartime: Forced Labour and Displacement

By 1942, Yevdokia was in German-occupied territory, registered under what appears to be an assumed or assigned identity. A wartime labour register — the type now held by the Arolsen Archives — records her as entry #69:

1942 German Labour Register — Entry #69

Name: Hermaniuk, Stefanie
Occupation: Arbeiterin (worker)
Born: 14.2.22
Birthplace: Rudniki
Marital status: Ledig (single)
Religion: Catholic
Nationality: Ukr.

The birth day (14 February) matches Yevdokia's known birthday. The year 1922 is one year later than the working assumption of 1921 — a common discrepancy in wartime documents. "Hermaniuk" is a cover surname, not a family name; "Rudniki" a cover birthplace. The entry marks the earliest appearance of the identity she would carry into Belgium.

How and when she and Vladimir were separated from Soviet territory, whether through capture, forced labour deportation, or flight, remains undocumented. What is clear is that by the summer of 1944, Vladimir was in the Verviers region of eastern Belgium.

The Partisans: Front de l'Indépendance

Vladimir Snisarenko enlisted in the Belgian resistance in the early days of August 1944, joining Corps O/14 of the Armée Belge des Partisans, based in Verviers, under the umbrella of the Front de l'Indépendance. A group commendation letter confirms he was one of nine men who enlisted together, all assessed as having conduct "toujours parfaite" — always perfect.

He rose to a leadership role. The reverse of a group photograph, taken at a Verviers studio, is inscribed in Russian: "Группа Русских Партизан Под Командою Снисаренко Владимира" — "A group of Russian partisans under the command of Snisarenko Vladimir." The photograph shows approximately thirteen men, several in berets, at least one wearing an armband reading "L'INDEP..." — consistent with the Front de l'Indépendance.

Partisan Demobilisation Card

Armée Belge des Partisans, Corps O/14, Verviers
Name: Snisorenko Wladimir
Demobilised: 11 November 1944
Conduct: Très bonne (very good)
Signed by the Commandant adjoint du Corps. Photograph of a young man affixed.

US Army Reference Letter — 19 May 1945

Written by Lt. George R. Hanson, 492nd Reinforcement Company, APO #131. Names "Vladimir Snisarnko" as a Russian soldier who had been with the unit since 18 April 1945 and was being released to return to Belgium. The letter notes he had hoped to return to Russia but did not — consistent with his decision to remain in the West and rebuild his life under a new identity.

Belgium: A New Identity

After the war, Vladimir and Yevdokia faced a choice shared by thousands of displaced Soviet citizens: return to the USSR, where former POWs and forced labourers faced suspicion and worse, or stay in the West and start over. They chose to stay — and to become other people.

Vladimir became Victor Wychenewsky, born 6 May 1916 in Krakow, nationality Polish. Yevdokia became Stefa Hermaniuk, born 4 February 1922 in Krakow, also Polish. The constructed identities were internally consistent: Polish-born Greek Catholics with Ukrainian roots, a plausible and unexceptional background in postwar Belgium.

They married officially on 25 May 1946 in the commune of Xhendelesse in the province of Liège, in a civil ceremony followed by a Greek Catholic religious ceremony officiated by l'Abbé Jean Tetierz. Their son, Georges Yvan Vladimir, was born in Liège on 15 June 1946 — his middle name "Vladimir" quietly honouring his father's true name. Georges was baptised on 20 June 1946 and confirmed on 7 January 1947, both in the Greek Catholic rite.

Livret de Mariage — Xhendelesse, 25 May 1946

Civil marriage record of Victor Wychenensky and Stefa Hermaniuk. Lists the groom's father as "Wychenensky Peter" — a detail that would later conflict with a 1948 certificate naming the father as "Jefrin," an unresolved discrepancy within the constructed identity.

Certificat d'identité, de civisme — Mélen, 24 February 1948

Name: Victor Wychenewsky
Occupation: Mineur (miner)
Identity card: No. 285
Born: Krakow, 5 May 1916
Nationality: Polonaise
Resident in Mélen since: 27 May 1947 (previously in Xhendelesse)

Victor worked as a coal miner in the Liège basin. His signature on this document reads approximately "Wysnesky" — a transitional spelling between the Belgian cover name and the eventual Australian surname Wiszniewski.

This was understood by the family to be their second, official wedding — not their first. A private photograph inscription in which Yevdokia addresses Vladimir as "мужу" (husband) and signs as "жены" (wife), using their original names, suggests they considered themselves already married before the Belgian ceremony. Whether a prior registered marriage took place in the Soviet Union has not been established.

Australia: The Wiszniewski Family

The family emigrated from Belgium to Australia, eventually settling in Georges Hall, a suburb in south-western Sydney, New South Wales. At some point during or after the migration, the surname underwent its final transformation: from Wychenewsky/Wychenensky through transitional forms to Wiszniewski.

Australian migration records for the family have not yet been investigated and remain an open research thread.

The private life of the family continued in Russian. The photograph inscriptions, the pet names — Georges was called "Гришенька" (Grishenka) within the family, and Yevdokia was "Дусенька" (Dusen'ka) — and the dedications were all written in a language the public identities did not speak. The original names survived in private, even as the public world knew only the Wiszniewskis.

Darya Andreevna Gamayunova, Yevdokia's mother, died on 19 December 1983 in Kirovograd, apparently never having left the Soviet Union. Her gravestone dedication — "from daughter and sister" — confirms that the family tie endured across decades and borders, even if reunion proved impossible.

The Documentary Record

The strength of this family's story lies in the unusual completeness of its paper trail. The collection spans three countries, four decades, two alphabets, and two complete identity sets. Below are the key inscriptions from the private photographs — the documents that bind the public record to the private truth.

Photo-001 — Inscription (reverse)

"На память моему мужу Снисаренко Владимиру от жены Гамаюновой Евдокии Матвеевны. Береги моё фото, как берёг и любил меня. Крепкий поцелуй."

"In remembrance, to my husband Vladimir Snisarenko, from his wife Yevdokia Matveevna Gamayunova. Keep my photograph as you cherished and loved me. A loving kiss."

This single inscription names both grandparents under their original, pre-Wiszniewski identities, in Yevdokia's hand.

Photo-002 — Inscription (reverse)

"Гришеньке на память. Это твоя мама, когда ей было 19 лет."

"For Grishenka, as a keepsake. This is your mama when she was 19 years old."

If the subject is Yevdokia (born c. 1921), the portrait dates to approximately 1940, before her wartime displacement. "Grishenka" is the family's private diminutive for their son Georges.

Photo-007 — Partisan Group Photograph (reverse)

"Группа Русских Партизан Под Командою [Лейтенанта?] Снисаренко Владимира"

"A group of Russian partisans under the command of [Lieutenant?] Snisarenko Vladimir."

Studio stamp on front: "PHOTO CH. FARMENTIER, 126 Rue de Dison, VERVIERS" — placing production in Verviers, consistent with the Corps O/14 base.

Third Photograph Inscription (tentative transcription)

"На память, на долгие годы, моим дорогим — Дусенька, Владимир, и [сынишке?] Гришенька! 26 [июня?] 1948. Без слов — от сердца."

"In remembrance, for the years to come, to my beloved ones — Dusen'ka, Vladimir, and little son(?) Grishenka! 26 June(?) 1948. Without words — from the heart."

This inscription addresses all three family members by their genuine private names in 1948. The writer must be someone outside the household who knew their real identities — a fellow émigré, comrade, or relative. A higher-resolution photograph is needed to confirm the reading and identify the writer.

Name Variants Across Documents

The grandfather's surname appears in three genuinely distinct spellings across independent original documents. These are authentic variants produced by different scribes in different languages, not transcription errors — all must remain active in any archive search.

Open Research Questions

This history remains a work in progress. Several threads of inquiry are still open, and the family welcomes contact from researchers, archivists, or anyone who recognises a name or a face.

Identity and Origins

  • Where and when was Vladimir Snisarenko born? The cover identity uses Krakow, 1916, but this is a fabrication.
  • Was there a registered marriage in the Soviet Union before the 1946 Belgian ceremony?
  • Who is the "sister" named on Darya Andreevna's gravestone? An unidentified maternal-line relative.

Wartime and Displacement

  • How and when did Yevdokia and Vladimir leave Soviet territory — through capture, forced labour deportation, or flight?
  • The FI Corps O/14 commendation letter's date contains a Roman numeral month (II or XI) — the original needs rechecking.
  • Who wrote the third photograph inscription (c. June 1948), addressing the family by their private names? A comrade, relative, or fellow émigré?

Archives and Records

  • Kirovohrad Oblast State Archive: a request covering fond R-7915 (civil status acts) and fond P-5907 (filtration files) has been submitted. Fond R-7915, Опис 1, Справа 5 has been partially searched without a matching entry.
  • CegeSoma (Belgium): an initial search returned "no file found" — re-searching under all surname variants (Snisarenko, Snisorenko, Snisarnko, Wychenewsky) is planned.
  • Australian migration records remain entirely uninvestigated.
  • The Arolsen Archives should be searched directly (browser-only) under each surname spelling variant.
